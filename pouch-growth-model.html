Notes

Limits worth knowing before quoting anything

  • On a flat budget a gain is a gain, wherever it comes from. Set the ramp to 0% and a 25% gain in revenue terms on any lever returns 25% more revenue. AOV and acquisition efficiency stay exactly equal to each other at every ramp, because both are straight multipliers on revenue. What separates the levers is everything below.
  • A growing budget discounts retention. Retention pays out months after a customer is bought, and a climbing budget means the cohort that pays it back is smaller than the one being bought today. At a 12% monthly ramp a retention gain delivers about four fifths of what an equally sized AOV or efficiency gain delivers; at 20% about two thirds; at 0% they are identical. The figure under the ramp slider shows this live.
  • Subscribers and non-subscribers are two different populations, not one spread. In the mature Shopify cohort, subscribers average 5.72 lifetime orders and are still reordering at roughly 80% per step. Non-subscribers average 1.38, and four in five never place a second order. That is why retention is split into two levers rather than one blended number: attach rate is how many new buyers start a subscription, subscriber lifetime is how long they then stay. They are different work by different people, and they compound.
  • The retention levers have the most room left, but they are the slowest to pay. Doubling attach from 27% to 50% lifts blended lifetime orders about 40%, more than either AOV or efficiency can offer inside their ceilings. At the default 12% ramp and a 12-month target attach reaches $100K on its own without passing its ceiling, where AOV and acquisition efficiency each need about +26% against a +25% ceiling. But pull the horizon in to 9 months and the retention levers become the worst of the four, because they compound and need months of cohorts behind them. On a flat budget no single lever gets there at all.
  • One lever at a time understates the combined case. The levers multiply rather than add. The combined line shows that; the four single-lever lines deliberately do not.
  • Ad spend only. Agency, production and influencer cost are excluded, so "contribution after ad spend" is gross profit minus media and nothing else. Pouch's own scorecard loads roughly $13,869 a month of them in, which is why their internal CAC reads near $150 for August against the $115 planning figure. These numbers are not comparable to that view.
  • The cohort figures are floors, not final. Pouch launched in January 2026, so even the mature cohort has at most nine months of history. Subscribers were still continuing at about 80% per order step when the data ran out, so 5.72 will keep climbing. Non-subscribers at 1.38 are close to final, because their dropoff happens immediately. Worth refreshing every quarter. Because 5.72 is a floor, the subscriber lifetime lever is measuring improvement from a conservative base.
  • Gross profit scales with AOV at a fixed margin. True when the basket grows, optimistic when AOV rises on discounting. The $37.76 it derives from is a non-subscriber figure applied to every order, including discounted subscription ones.
  • A better mix applies only to customers acquired from here on. Everyone already on the books keeps today's behaviour, which is why every line starts together. Reactivating lapsed customers is upside this does not count.
  • Powder is not modeled separately. The Q1 launch changes AOV, CAC, attach and subscriber lifetime. Because the engine runs on those inputs rather than on a product, powder is a new set of values rather than a rebuild.
  • No seasonality and no competitive response. BFCM, category crowding and CPM inflation are all flat here.
